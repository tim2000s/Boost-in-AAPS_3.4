# How carbohydrate is announced, and what that means for reading meals from glucose

A synthesis of the scripts in this folder. Every figure below is produced by one of them and can be
regenerated by running it; nothing here is quoted from elsewhere.

| script | what it answers |
|---|---|
| `meal_clock.py` | when carbohydrate is announced and how much, hour by hour |
| `meal_size_distribution.py` | the shape of the size distribution across all reported entries |
| `meal_windows.py` | the three meal windows, split by study |
| `commons_meal_windows.py` | the same, on the OpenAPS Data Commons |
| `repeat_entries.py` | entries close together, whether they repeat, and what a cluster totals |
| `extract_meals_merged.py` | whether counting meals properly makes their size readable |

## The short version

Timing carries real structure. Size carries almost none, and what looks like size is mostly the
identity of the person and the hour of the day. Neither of those changed when we corrected a defect
in how a meal was counted, though the correction matters for how every size in this programme
should be described.

The more useful result is about the data rather than the model. What these corpora record is not a
sequence of meals. It is a sequence of entries, and the gap between the two is large enough to
change the median meal size by about a fifth.

## Timing has structure; size does not

Across 564,201 announcements from 1,028 participants, placed on a single 24-hour axis, the day has
three clear peaks: 11:00, 12:00 and 18:00. The six hours from midnight carry 5 per cent of all
announcements.

The size distribution barely moves against that. Hour by hour the median runs between 20 and 33 g
across the entire day, and the interquartile range is 10 to 20 g wide in every single hour. The
10th centile sits at 10 to 14 g throughout. Knowing the hour tells you when somebody eats and close
to nothing about how much.

Individuals are far less regular than the pooled picture suggests. The median participant announces
29 per cent of their carbohydrate inside their own three busiest hours, across a range of 0.18 to
0.67 over 996 participants with enough data. The structure in the figure is population-level, not
personal, which is consistent with the per-user anticipation shadow having failed to beat a plain
hour-of-day rate.

## The three meal windows are nearly the same distribution, and the pooled answer is one study's

Breakfast 06:00 to 09:00, lunch 12:00 to 15:00, dinner 17:00 to 20:00.

| study | breakfast | lunch | dinner | modal size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loop | 24 g | 23 g | 25 g | about 15 g |
| ReplaceBG | 32 g | 35 g | 40 g | about 30 g |

Pooled, the median is an identical 25 g in all three windows. That figure is Loop's: it contributes
90 per cent of the entries. Loop is genuinely flat across the day. ReplaceBG rises steadily from
breakfast to dinner, a span of 8 g, and sits higher and broader throughout.

The difference is not age. Loop's median participant is 27 against ReplaceBG's 44, and Loop includes
children where ReplaceBG has nobody under 19, so age was the obvious candidate. Within Loop the
pattern is not even monotonic: adolescents announce the largest meals at 35 g and under-12s the
smallest at 22, with every adult band flat at 25. Matched tightly to 30 to 55 and using per-subject
medians, Loop is 30 g against ReplaceBG's 38. The gap survives age matching.

On the OpenAPS Data Commons, where announcements are derived from step increases in carbohydrate on
board rather than recorded directly, OpenAPS oref0 SMB is flat like Loop at 30, 29 and 30 g across
the windows. The two smaller platform groups show wider spans on a tenth of the sample and are
noisy for it.

## What people actually type

The distribution of all 1,617,910 reported entries is not a bell curve and is not log-normal
either. Skew is +1.70 and excess kurtosis +5.38, against 0 and 0 for a normal. A normal of the same
mean and standard deviation peaks at 29 g where the data peaks near 15 to 20, and places mass below
zero. A log-normal peaks far too early at about 10 g and then overshoots the tail. On a log scale
the skew is -1.07.

At one-gram resolution the whole distribution is a comb. 72 per cent of announcements are a
multiple of 5 g and 43 per cent a multiple of 10, and the six commonest values account for 46 per
cent of everything entered. So before anyone asks whether a model can predict carbohydrate, the
target itself has a resolution of about 5 g.

Announced carbohydrate totals a median of 65 g per participant-day, and 89 per cent of participants
announce under 100 g. That is well below likely intake, so a large part of what these participants
ate was never announced at all.

## Entries are not meals

Asked whether people repeat the same amount inside an hour in the meal windows, the answer is yes
but slightly: 6.4 per cent of entries have a same-size partner within the hour, against 3.7 per cent
when each participant's own sizes are shuffled across their own timestamps. The excess is 2.7
percentage points. Without that control the raw rate reads as a strong pattern and is almost
entirely coincidence, because six amounts account for nearly half of all entries.

The broader pattern is more consequential. Of successive entries inside half an hour, 87 per cent
differ in size, and the second is usually smaller. Chaining entries within half an hour and treating
each chain as one eating occasion:

| chain threshold | single entry | two | three or more | entries absorbed | median total of a multi-entry cluster |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 15 min | 92% | 7% | 1% | 16% | 50 g |
| 30 min | 87% | 11% | 2% | 25% | 53 g |
| 60 min | 81% | 15% | 4% | 36% | 56 g |

For a quarter of entries the eating occasion is roughly twice the size the entry records.

## The target was wrong, and correcting it changes nothing

The readability extraction keeps the first entry of a cluster and skips anything within ninety
minutes, recording that first entry's grams as the meal. It does not merge. That sets aside 26 per
cent of entries and 22 per cent of all carbohydrate, and for the 28 per cent of meals that lose
something the recorded size is a median 25 g against 55 g for the occasion it belongs to.

`extract_meals_merged.py` rebuilds the extraction with that one change, importing every filter, the
onset rule and the features from the original so nothing else can move. Meal boundaries are
identical and both produce exactly 492,440 Loop meals. The median meal goes from 28 g to 34 g.

| horizon | trace over the clock, original | merged | trace over person, clock and history, original | merged |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 min | +0.008 | +0.006 | +0.021 | +0.032 |
| 20 min | +0.011 | +0.008 | +0.021 | +0.032 |
| 60 min | +0.053 | +0.054 | +0.026 | +0.041 |

The pre-registered margin was 0.05 at twenty minutes or less. Nothing clears it on either target.
On quantity, the model matches each participant's own median on both definitions and beats it on
neither: 13.1 g against 13.0 originally, 17.0 against 17.0 merged.

The reason the original held is not that the target was noisy. Forty grams of difference moves the
ten-minute rise by under 1 mg/dL against a between-meal spread of about ten, and merging changes
the target without touching that ratio.

## What follows

Nothing here reopens the size question. It closes it more firmly, because the most plausible
remaining objection, that the target was mis-specified, has now been tested and rejected on the
same events with the same model.

Two things change. Every size figure this programme has quoted is a first entry rather than an
eating occasion, and should be described that way. And the clock is worth keeping in view for
detection rather than sizing: announcements are strongly peaked through the day while undeclared
rises are nearly flat, which is a separation a detector could use and currently does not.
